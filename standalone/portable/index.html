Chapter 1: Introduction ​

1.1 The Battery Regulation and this guideline ​

Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 of 12 July 2023 concerns batteries and waste batteries. It replaces the former Batteries Directive (Directive 2006/66/EC) and creates one framework for the whole battery lifecycle: sustainability and safety requirements, labelling and information, conformity assessment, battery due diligence, extended producer responsibility, and the collection, treatment and recycling of waste batteries. It applies to all batteries, regardless of their shape, volume, weight, design, material composition, chemistry, use or purpose, subject to its express exclusions (Article 1(3), (5)–(6)).

The Regulation applies in stages. It has applied generally since 18 February 2024, while many provisions apply from later dates. Some of these dates depend on secondary legislation, such as delegated and implementing acts. Chapter 14 lists the dates that matter for portable batteries.

For portable batteries, including portable batteries of general use, the Regulation covers the following topics. Each is explained in a chapter of this guideline:

  • product requirements, such as restricted substances and, for portable batteries of general use, performance and durability (chapter 2);
  • the responsibilities of manufacturers, importers, distributors and other actors (chapter 3);
  • labels and the QR code that gives access to information (chapter 4);
  • batteries that are incorporated in products, including removal and replacement by the end-user (chapter 5);
  • battery due diligence (chapter 8);
  • producer registration, extended producer responsibility and reporting (chapter 9);
  • collection, treatment, recycling and re-use (chapters 10 and 11);
  • the data that describes a battery, attribute by attribute (chapter 15).

In practice, operators need reliable data about batteries, the parties involved and the events that occur across the value chain. This calls for common identifiers, clear relationships between data and interoperable ways to access and exchange information.

This guideline explains what the Regulation requires for portable batteries and how GS1 standards support the identification, information access, data exchange and evidence functions that follow from those requirements. It can be read on its own.

The guideline is meant for manufacturers, importers, distributors, producers and other professional operators responsible for portable batteries, together with the teams and service providers that implement their identification and information systems.

The Regulation requires particular functions and outcomes. It does not prescribe GS1 standards. Where this guideline describes the use of GS1 standards, it proposes an interoperable way to implement a function. Using a GS1 standard does not by itself demonstrate legal compliance, and the Regulation and its secondary acts always prevail. This guideline provides implementation guidance, not a complete system specification or an assessment of an individual operator's compliance.

1.3 Applicable outcome ​

Portable batteries, including portable batteries of general use, do not require an Article 77(1) battery passport. From 18 February 2027 they follow the Article 13(6)(b) QR-information route.

The Regulation requires identification, information access and evidence but does not prescribe GS1 standards for these functions. This guideline proposes GS1 identification, GS1 Digital Link and relevant data-exchange standards.

GTIN identifies a battery trade item; batch/lot and serial qualifiers distinguish production batches and individual instances. GLN identifies parties and locations, SSCC identifies logistic units, and EPCIS captures and shares visibility events. The following chapters explain the contribution and limits of each component.

A QR code, its encoded identifier, the information service, physical labels and CE marking have different functions; a QR code alone is not a passport. Chapter 1.4 gives the category test, and chapter 16 explains the terms used here.

The scope exclusions in Article 1(5) remain applicable. For equipment specifically designed for the safety of nuclear installations, Article 1(6) excludes Chapters III and IX of the Regulation, which cover labelling, marking and information, including the QR code, and the digital battery passport. Chapter VIII of the Regulation on waste batteries still applies. Assess the remaining provisions separately for that configuration.

1.4 How to read this guideline ​

The guideline follows the questions an operator needs to answer. What does the Regulation require? Who is responsible? Which battery and which information are concerned? How do GS1 standards support the implementation? Where a chapter proposes the use of GS1 standards, it separates what the Regulation requires from what this guideline proposes, and it states the limits of the standard.

How this guideline was created. This guideline is one of five category guidelines: portable batteries, light means of transport (LMT) batteries, starting, lighting and ignition (SLI) batteries, industrial batteries and electric vehicle (EV) batteries. All five are based on the GS1 in Europe master guideline, which covers every battery category. Each category guideline takes the common sections of the master and its category-specific section and presents what applies to that category as a document that can be read on its own. Because they share one basis, the five guidelines use the same structure, terms and conventions.

Check the category first. This guideline applies to portable batteries: batteries that are sealed, weigh 5 kg or less, are not specifically designed for industrial use and are neither EV, LMT nor SLI batteries (Article 3(1), point (9)). Confirm the category before relying on the guideline; chapter 16 gives the definition of each category. A battery that falls into another category, such as an LMT, industrial or electric vehicle battery, is assessed under that category, and a battery passport may then apply.

2.3 Carbon footprint ​

Article 7 does not currently apply to portable batteries. It applies to EV batteries, LMT batteries and rechargeable industrial batteries above 2 kWh. No carbon-footprint declaration, class or threshold is therefore required for portable batteries (Article 7).

2.4 Recovered-material content ​

Article 8 does not currently apply to portable batteries (Article 8(1)–(3)).

2.5 Performance and durability ​

A portable battery of general use is a portable-battery subcategory. It comprises batteries specifically designed to be interoperable and having one of the formats listed in Article 3(1), point (10): 4.5 V (3R12), button cell, D, C, AA, AAA, AAAA, A23 or 9 V (PP3). It covers rechargeable and non-rechargeable batteries. The interoperable design and the listed format are cumulative: a battery is not within the subcategory merely because it is portable or has a similar use.

From 18 August 2028 or 24 months after the Article 9(2) delegated act enters into force, whichever is later, manufacturers must ensure that portable batteries of general use other than button cells meet the minimum values set by that act for the applicable Annex III parameters (Article 9(1)–(2)).

The Article 9(2) delegated act has not been reviewed for this guideline (OQ-REG-015), so verify its minimum values, measurement conditions and resulting date before setting operational requirements. Article 9(3) requires the Commission to assess, by 31 December 2030, measures concerning non-rechargeable portable batteries of general use; it does not itself prohibit or phase out those batteries. Apart from Article 9 and the separate reporting in chapter 9, portable batteries of general use follow the same requirements as other portable batteries, with the same passport outcome and QR route.

3.2 Manufacturer duties ​

Manufacturers must (Article 38(1)–(8)):

  • ensure compliance with the applicable requirements, which for portable batteries means Article 6 and, for portable batteries of general use, Article 9;
  • provide clear instructions and safety information in the required language or languages;
  • apply the required markings and labels (chapter 4);
  • draw up technical documentation and carry out, or have carried out, the applicable conformity assessment before placing the battery on the market or putting it into service;
  • after conformity is demonstrated, draw up the EU declaration of conformity and affix the CE marking;
  • maintain procedures so that series-produced batteries remain conforming; and
  • provide the required battery identification and manufacturer contact information, namely name, registered trade name or trade mark, postal address, and a website and email address if available (Article 38(6)–(7)).

Cell and module suppliers must provide manufacturers, free of charge, with the information and documentation needed for compliance (Article 39).

3.3 When another actor becomes the manufacturer ​

An importer or distributor is treated as the manufacturer if it places a battery on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trade mark, modifies a battery already on the market so that compliance could be affected, or changes its purpose (Article 44). An operator carrying out preparation for re-use, preparation for repurposing, repurposing or remanufacturing and then placing the battery on the market or putting it into service is also treated as the manufacturer (Articles 38(11) and 45; chapter 11).

3.5 Technical documentation ​

Before placing a battery on the market or putting it into service, the manufacturer draws up documentation that enables conformity assessment and contains an adequate risk analysis. Depending on the module and the applicable requirements, it includes the description and intended use; design and manufacturing material; a specimen of the required label; applied standards and specifications; alternative solutions; calculations and evidence; and test reports (Article 38(2); Annex VIII, Parts A–C, point 2). It must be retrievable and supplied to authorities on request (Articles 38(10), 41(7)–(8) and 42(6)).

The Regulation does not prescribe an identifier for this evidence. GTIN associates it with the model it covers, and GTIN plus serial number associates it with one unit where needed.

3.6 EU declaration of conformity ​

The declaration states that conformity with the applicable product requirements has been demonstrated. It follows Annex IX, includes the applicable Annex VIII elements, is kept current, is translated as required by the Member State, and is electronic, with paper supplied on request. Where several Union acts require declarations, one declaration covers and identifies them. By drawing it up, the manufacturer assumes responsibility. Annex VIII generally associates it with a model, and Module G associates it with the particular battery (Article 18; Annex VIII).

3.7 CE marking ​

CE marking shows conformity with applicable Union harmonisation legislation; it is not a battery, batch or party identifier. It must be visible, legible and indelible and be affixed before placing on the market or putting into service. If marking the battery is impossible or not warranted because of its nature, it goes on the packaging and accompanying documents. Where a notified body is required, its number follows the CE marking (Article 3(1), point (36); Articles 19–20).

3.9 Corrective action, withdrawal and recall ​

Manufacturers and importers that believe a battery they placed on the market is non-compliant must immediately correct, withdraw or recall it, as appropriate. If it presents a risk, they immediately inform the relevant authority, with details of the non-compliance and the action taken. Distributors ensure that appropriate action is taken and give a similar notification. A distributor or importer that believes a battery is non-compliant must not make it available or place it on the market until conformity is restored (Articles 38(9)–(10), 41(5)–(6) and (8), 42(3), (5)–(6) and 43).

Authorities may require correction, withdrawal or recall, and action extends to the affected batteries the operator made available throughout the Union. Measures can also apply to a compliant battery that presents a risk. Formal defects, including absent or improper CE marking, declaration or documentation, or missing manufacturer or importer details, must be corrected (Articles 79, 81 and 83). GS1 identifiers help locate the affected parties, models, batches or instances (chapter 12). They do not determine the remedy or prove conformity.

4.2 Placement ​

The labels and the QR code must be printed or engraved visibly, legibly and indelibly on the battery. Where this is not possible or not warranted because of the nature and size of the battery, they are affixed to the packaging and to the accompanying documents (Article 13(7)). CE marking and QR access are separate. CE marking is affixed under Articles 19–20 (chapter 3). The QR code gives access to the declaration of conformity under Article 13(6)(b). Neither replaces the other.

4.4 The QR code requirement ​

From 18 February 2027 all batteries must bear a QR code (Article 13(6)). For portable batteries, it gives access to the Article 13(6)(b) information. Portable batteries have no battery passport.

Chapter 5: Batteries incorporated in products and service relationships ​

Incorporation does not take a battery out of the Regulation (Article 1(3)). A battery in or with another product remains a separate regulated object, and its category and capacity decide its passport outcome, not the product it is in.

5.1 Removal and replacement by the end-user ​

From 18 February 2027, any person placing on the market a product incorporating a portable battery must ensure that the battery is readily removable and replaceable by the end-user throughout the product lifetime (Articles 11(1) and 96(2)(a)). This is not only a battery-manufacturer duty. The rule concerns the entire battery, not individual cells or other parts. Readily removable means removable with commercially available tools, without specialised tools unless supplied free with the product, proprietary tools, thermal energy or solvents.

The product must include instructions and safety information on battery use, removal and replacement, also permanently available on a public website in a form easily understood by end-users (Article 11(1)). The following duties also apply:

  • a replacement must be compatible without affecting functioning, performance or safety;
  • replacement batteries must be available as spare parts for at least five years after the last unit of the equipment model is placed on the market, at a reasonable and non-discriminatory price (Article 11(7)); and
  • software must not impede replacement with a compatible battery or key components (Article 11(8)).

5.2 Derogations and exclusions ​

The end-user requirement does not apply where continuity of power supply is necessary and a permanent connection between the product and the battery is required for the safety of the user and the appliance or, for products whose main function is collecting and supplying data, for data integrity (Article 11(3)).

5.4 How GS1 standards support service relationships ​

The Regulation requires removal, replacement and information duties but does not prescribe GS1 standards. GTIN identifies the battery model and a separate GTIN identifies the host product. A GS1 Digital Link URI gives access to instructions and safety information on use, removal and replacement. GLN identifies a party or service location, and GTIN plus serial number keeps track of the replacement and repair of individual batteries.

A battery GTIN does not identify the host model the battery is incorporated in, or record when the last unit of that model was placed on the market, which the spare-part period depends on. The instructions have to remain accessible after the ordinary commercial product page has been withdrawn (Article 11(1)).

Chapter 8: Battery due diligence ​

Battery due diligence depends on the economic operator, not on the battery passport. A portable battery is not excluded because it has no passport.

8.1 Who it applies to and from when ​

Chapter VII of the Regulation, on battery due diligence, applies to an economic operator that places batteries on the market or puts them into service, subject to Article 47. A covered operator must establish and implement a battery due-diligence policy addressing the raw materials and risks in Annex X and meet Articles 48–52, from 18 August 2027 (Article 48(1), as amended by Regulation (EU) 2025/1561).

Annex X lists cobalt, natural graphite, lithium, nickel and chemical compounds based on them that are necessary for manufacturing battery active materials. Its risk categories cover environment, climate and human health; human rights, labour rights and industrial relations; and community life, including that of indigenous peoples (Annex X, points 1–2). Article 48(8) permits amendments, so check the applicable delegated acts.

Two exclusions apply (Article 47):

  • Turnover. An operator is excluded only where both elements are met: it had net turnover below EUR 40 million in the financial year preceding the last financial year, and it is not part of a parent-and-subsidiary group whose consolidated turnover exceeds EUR 40 million. Retain evidence for both.
  • Circular operations. Chapter VII of the Regulation does not apply to batteries subjected to preparation for re-use, preparation for repurposing, repurposing or remanufacturing that had already been placed on the market or put into service before that operation. This covers those batteries only, not the operator’s other activities.

Chapter 9: Producer registration, EPR and market reporting ​

Producer registration and extended producer responsibility (EPR) apply to portable batteries, including those incorporated in appliances, light means of transport or other vehicles. They do not depend on the battery passport. The decisive questions are who first makes the battery available in a Member State, how it is supplied, and whether an authorised representative for EPR or a producer responsibility organisation acts for the producer (Article 3(1), points (47)–(49); Articles 55–58).

9.1 Who the producer is ​

“Producer” is a role determined separately for each Member State. It is not necessarily the manufacturer named on the battery. Article 3(1), point (47), covers a person that, irrespective of selling technique:

  • is established in a Member State and first supplies there batteries manufactured under its own name or trade mark;
  • is established in a Member State and first resells there, under its own name or trade mark, batteries made by another party whose name or trade mark does not appear;
  • is established in a Member State and first supplies there, on a professional basis, batteries from another Member State or a third country; or
  • is established in another Member State or a third country and sells batteries by distance contract directly to end-users in the Member State concerned.

A party can be the producer in one Member State without being the producer for the same battery in another. Maintain the determination and its evidence for each combination of legal entity, Member State and supply route. An operator that first makes available a battery resulting from a listed circular operation is the producer of that battery (Article 56(2)).

9.2 Registration ​

A producer must apply electronically in every Member State where it first makes a portable battery available, and may supply there only if it, or its EPR authorised representative, is registered (Article 55(1)–(2)). Before making a battery available, a distributor verifies that the producer is registered (Article 42(2)(a)).

The application includes the producer’s name, brand names, address and contact details; its national identification code, trade-register or equivalent official number and tax identification number; each relevant battery category and chemistry; information on the EPR, collection and data-reliability arrangements and any appointed producer responsibility organisation; and a statement that the information is true (Article 55(3)). EPR and collection information can instead be supplied through the Article 58 authorisation application. Member States may combine registration and authorisation, request additional documents, set procedures and charge proportionate fees.

Once the application is complete, the competent authority grants registration and provides a registration number within no more than 12 weeks (Article 55(9)(b)). The authority may refuse or withdraw registration where required information or evidence is absent or insufficient or the producer no longer meets the EPR requirements, and must withdraw it where the producer has ceased to exist (Article 55(11)). Changes and permanent cessation are notified without undue delay (Article 55(12)). Check current status, and do not treat possession of a number as permanent proof of compliance.

9.3 Producer responsibility and costs ​

A producer may appoint an authorised producer responsibility organisation (PRO) to fulfil EPR on its behalf, and a Member State may make this mandatory where justified (Article 57(1)). Contributions to a PRO are modulated at least by category and chemistry (Article 57(2)). A producer fulfilling EPR individually, or a PRO fulfilling it collectively, must obtain authorisation and provide the required guarantee (Article 58).

A producer selling directly by distance contract to end-users in a Member State from another Member State or a third country must appoint, by written mandate, an EPR authorised representative in every Member State of sale (Article 3(1), points (47)(d) and (48); Article 56(3)).

Online platforms within Section 4 of Chapter III of Regulation (EU) 2022/2065 (the Digital Services Act) that allow consumers to conclude distance contracts with producers must obtain the relevant producer register and registration number or numbers, plus the producer’s self-certification that it offers only batteries meeting the specified EPR requirements (Article 62(6)). If registration information is not public, Member States give qualifying platforms free access (Article 55(13)). Another identifier may be an internal lookup key but does not satisfy Article 62(6), and self-certification is not independent verification.

9.5 Reporting quantities ​

Producers or, where appointed, PROs report annually to the competent authority by category and chemistry, including batteries incorporated in vehicles and appliances. The report covers the amount first made available; amounts collected, delivered for treatment, exported for treatment or specified circular operations, and delivered for preparation for re-use or repurposing; and the collection rate (Article 75(1), (4)). Collection rates use the three-year-average method in Annex XI. Portable batteries of general use are reported as a separate amount (Article 75(1)(b)). This does not create a separate collection target for them; Article 94(2)(e) instead requires the Commission’s review to consider one.

Reporting is territorial: batteries that leave the Member State in the same year before sale to end-users are excluded from the amount first made available. Reports are due within six months after the reporting year. The first reporting year is 2026, with reports due by 30 June 2027. This is calculated from Article 75(7) and Article 2 of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2289, which entered into force on 11 December 2025.

Article 1 of that implementing act sets the format Member States use to report to the Commission and does not directly address operators. Operators report in the format set by the competent authorities of each Member State where they have reporting duties (Article 75(8)), so check the current requirements of those authorities or of the PRO. The Commission format is still useful for planning, because it shows the data Member States have to supply (Article 76(1); Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2289, Annexes I to III).

Chapter 10: Collection, treatment and recycling ​

These rules apply to waste portable batteries, whether or not a battery passport applied.

10.1 Collection and take-back ​

Producers or appointed producer responsibility organisations (PROs) must establish free, territory-wide separate collection through connected points and deliver the batteries to a permitted treatment facility, covering batteries regardless of nature, chemistry, condition, brand or origin (Article 59(1), (2), (4)–(6)).

Distributors must take back qualifying waste batteries free of charge and without requiring a new purchase, at or in the immediate vicinity of the retail outlet, and in quantities normally discarded by non-professional end-users. The duty is limited to categories they offer or have offered and does not apply to waste products containing batteries (Article 62(1)–(2)). They hand the batteries to the responsible producer or PRO or to a selected waste management operator (Article 62(3)). Distance-selling distributors provide sufficient collection points, and for sales with delivery they offer free take-back and inform the end-user when ordering (Article 62(4)–(5)). End-users discard waste batteries separately from other waste (Article 64).

Distributors supplying end-users provide it permanently in their premises, easily accessible and clearly visible, with information on returning waste batteries free of charge, also when selling through online platforms (Article 74(4)). Producers or PROs conduct awareness campaigns and offer incentives to encourage correct discarding (Article 74(6)). Commercially sensitive information stays confidential (Article 74(7)).

Producers also give distributors and waste operators information on safety and protective measures for storage and collection. They give operators carrying out preparation or treatment model-specific dismantling and safety information, including components, materials and hazardous-substance locations, electronically, free of charge and on request (Article 74(2)–(3)).

10.3 Storage, transport, handover and treatment ​

Collection infrastructure has to suit the expected volume and hazardous nature of the batteries. Transport containers must meet Directive 2008/68/EC (Article 59(1)(c)). At treatment facilities, impermeable surfaces and weatherproof covering or suitable containers are required, and batteries must not be mixed with conductive or combustible waste. Lithium-based batteries require heat, water, damage, orientation, ventilation, isolation and warning controls (Annex XII, Part A, points 2–4).

Operators of treatment facilities for end-of-life vehicles and waste electrical and electronic equipment (WEEE) hand removed batteries to the relevant producer, PRO or selected waste operator and keep transaction records (Article 65). What these records contain beyond what is expressly listed is open (OQ-REG-014). An operational record normally holds date, source, destination, parties, category, chemistry, measured quantity, logistic-unit reference and intended operation; these are implementation data, not additional legal requirements.

Separately collected batteries must undergo treatment and must not be disposed of or sent to energy recovery. Treatment takes place in a permitted facility, complies with Annex XII, Part A, and uses best available techniques under Directive 2010/75/EU. It removes fluids and acids, and mercury and cadmium are each collected into identifiable streams. Batteries are removed from WEEE, end-of-life vehicles and other waste products during treatment (Articles 70–71; Annex XII, Part A).

10.8 Mixed and damaged streams ​

Collection covers batteries regardless of condition. SSCC identifies a mixed container, and records associate it with measured or estimated content by category and chemistry. After sorting, new logistic units and measured contents can be related to the incoming unit. Damaged, leaking or thermally affected batteries are segregated and recorded at the level needed for safety and transport controls. If an original label, serial number or QR code is unreadable, an operator must not create an unverified link. A local consignment identifier can preserve custody while recording that the original identity is unknown.

10.9 Shipments of used and waste batteries ​

Waste batteries or their fractions may be treated outside the Member State concerned or outside the Union, provided that the shipment complies with Regulations (EC) No 1013/2006 and (EC) No 1418/2007 (Article 72(1)). Chapter 11: Re-use and other circular operations ​

11.1 The operations ​

The Regulation distinguishes the operations by waste status, intended purpose and technical outcome (Article 3(1), points (29)–(32)):

OperationWhat it means
Re-useA used battery used again. It does not itself amount to a new placing on the market
Preparation for re-useA waste battery prepared for use again without other pre-processing
Preparation for repurposingA waste battery prepared for a different purpose or application
RepurposingA battery that is not waste, used for a different purpose or application
RemanufacturingA used battery disassembled and evaluated at cell and module level, restored to at least 90% of original rated capacity, with no more than 3% difference in state of health between individual cells, for its original purpose or application

A battery intended for industrial use after preparation for repurposing or repurposing is an industrial battery (Article 3(1), point (13)). Classify the battery after the operation: its category and intended use may change the requirements and the passport outcome. If it then falls in a category with a passport, a passport applies.

11.2 Placing on the market again ​

A used battery undergoing only re-use is considered to have been placed on the market when first made available. A battery subjected to preparation for re-use, preparation for repurposing, repurposing or remanufacturing is placed on the market anew (Article 38(11), read with recital 16). An imported used battery is placed on the Union market when it first enters the Union, and an imported battery that has undergone a listed operation must comply with the applicable requirements on entering the Union (recital 16). The original conformity assessment, category and passport outcome cannot simply be assumed to remain sufficient.

11.3 Responsibility ​

An operator that carries out a listed operation and places the resulting battery on the market or puts it into service is considered a manufacturer (Article 38(11)). It performs the applicable manufacturer duties, including technical documentation, conformity assessment with an additional Module A assessment, the EU declaration of conformity, CE marking, identification, instructions, safety information and labelling (Articles 17–20 and 38). Examination, performance testing, packing and shipment follow adequate quality-control and safety instructions. The operator complies with the Regulation and relevant Union product, environmental, health and transport-safety law, taking account of any category change (Article 45). A remanufacturing operator gives authorities, on request, documentation that meets Article 3(1), point (32).

The due-diligence rules in Chapter VII of the Regulation do not apply to batteries already placed on the market before the listed operation (Article 47), but this does not remove the duties under Articles 38 and 45.

11.4 Labels, QR code and passport ​

Article 13(9) requires a battery subjected to a listed operation to bear new labels or markings with information on the change of status, accessible through the QR code. Annex XIII, point 4(c), lists “original”, “repurposed”, “re-used”, “remanufactured” and “waste”.

This does not create an Article 77 passport for portable batteries. Which information has to be accessible, at what granularity and from when is unresolved (OQ-REG-007). Maintaining a link to the original battery is an operational measure, not a passport duty. It supports conformity evidence, status history, safety investigations and recall scope.

Article 73 does not provide a general end-of-waste test for portable batteries.

Chapter 12: Supply-chain records and information exchange ​

12.1 What the Regulation requires ​

Economic operators must, on request of a national authority, provide market surveillance authorities with the identity of any economic operator that supplied them with a battery and of any operator they supplied, with the quantity and exact models. They must be able to do so for ten years after being supplied and ten years after supplying (Article 46). This applies independently of the battery passport, and neither a passport nor a due-diligence system replaces these records. Recital 76 explains the purpose: tracing operators makes market surveillance simpler and more efficient.

A minimal record preserves the supplying and receiving operators, the exact battery model and the quantity. Transaction date, document reference, ship-from and ship-to location, batch/lot or serial identifier, logistic-unit identifier and event time aid retrieval, reconciliation and recall, but Article 46 does not expressly require them. It requires operator and model traceability, not consumer identity or universal serialisation.

12.2 Critical Tracking Events and Key Data Elements ​

The Regulation does not prescribe GS1 standards. This guideline proposes the following approach. A traceability system is built from Critical Tracking Events (CTEs), such as transformation, shipment and receipt, and Key Data Elements (KDEs), which capture who, what, where, when and why. In GS1, GLN identifies the who and where. GTIN, with batch/lot number or serial number, identifies the what, and SSCC identifies a logistic unit. For Article 46, a GLN works as a party key linked to the name, address and evidence of the operator’s identity (GS1 Global Location Number Data Model Solution Standard, sections 4–5, pp. 11–50). It does not replace an authority-issued identifier.

Shipping and receiving are separate observations; one does not prove the other. Define how discrepancies, partial receipts, returns and reading errors are corrected. An SSCC does not identify its batteries unless the content relationship is also recorded. Assembly in which components remain recognisable is not automatically a transformation, so select event types and CBV values from their full definitions. Storage and transport under an importer’s, distributor’s or fulfilment provider’s responsibility must not jeopardise conformity (Articles 41(4), 42(4) and 43). EPCIS carries condition information where needed, but the Regulation does not impose continuous monitoring or require EPCIS.

12.4 Using EPCIS and GDSN ​

An EPCIS implementation should:

  1. identify the regulatory function or operational need, actor and period;
  2. select model, batch, instance, packaging or logistic-unit granularity, without using GTIN alone for an individual battery;
  3. identify the relevant parties, functions and locations, using GLNs where selected;
  4. define CTEs and KDEs, including quantity, corrections, returns, splits, aggregation and transformation;
  5. map processes to EPCIS event types and CBV vocabulary after checking their complete definitions;
  6. define capture, validation, storage, query, authentication, authorisation and retention; and
  7. test that records answer the relevant request, including Article 46 throughout its ten-year periods.

EPCIS supports capture and query interfaces, access control and redaction, and it does not require one database (GS1 EPCIS Standard, version 2.0.1, sections 8.1–8.2, pp. 103–145). The Regulation does not require it. EPCIS supports visibility and evidence management; it does not replace conformity assessment, audit, verification, certification or an authority decision.

Where GDSN is considered, identify whether each attribute describes the battery or a host product, the responsible data owner, target market, units, conditions and validation rules. The sources reviewed for this guideline do not establish that current GDSN attributes and target-market profiles cover every data element required by the Regulation.

12.5 Data quality, access and confidentiality ​

Traceability data has to be accurate, complete, timely and consistent enough for its function. Controls cover identifier validity, model and quantity reconciliation, duplicate events, late data, corrections, master-data changes and preservation (GS1 Global Traceability Standard, sections 3.3.1–3.3.5, pp. 18–21). Access is based on role and purpose. An Article 46 record has to be available to a market surveillance authority on request, but Article 46 does not require it to be public or prescribe a network architecture. Data-protection, confidentiality, cybersecurity and contractual requirements continue to apply.

12.6 Recall and withdrawal ​

Traceability records help determine affected models, batches or instances, identify recipients and quantities, locate stock, notify trading partners, reconcile returns and keep an auditable response record. Article 46 does not universally require batch or serial data. If records exist only at model level, the affected scope may have to include all units of that model supplied during the relevant period. Finer identification narrows an action only where reliable evidence confines both the issue and the distribution to that batch or those instances. Chapter 3 sets out the duties.

13.1 Rechargeable AA batteries in a retail pack ​

Assumed facts. A manufacturer places a retail pack of four rechargeable AA batteries on the Union market after 18 February 2027. The batteries are specifically designed to be interoperable and are portable batteries of general use under Article 3(1), point (10). Each battery is the same model and production batch, and the pack is the orderable retail trade item.

Regulatory outcome. The batteries do not require a passport under Article 77(1), and each battery is subject to the Article 13(6)(b) QR route. Because they are rechargeable, the capacity label applies from the conditional date in Article 13(2) and (10), and Article 13(3) does not apply. General labelling follows Article 13(1) and Annex VI. The separate-collection symbol and, where the threshold is exceeded, the chemical symbol also apply (Article 13(4)–(5)). The minimum values for the rechargeable Annex III parameters apply from the conditional date in Article 9(1)–(2). The producer includes the batteries in portable-battery collection and reporting and separately reports the amount of portable batteries of general use (Article 75(1)(b)).

GS1 implementation. GTIN identifies the retail pack. If the batteries are also traded separately or need their own information association, a separate GTIN identifies the battery trade item. GTIN plus batch/lot number links the batteries to batch-specific records. A GS1 Digital Link URI encoded in the required QR code is a web entry point to the Article 13(6)(b) resources, for example https://example.com/01/{GTIN}/10/{BATCH}.

Limits. This is an information service, not a passport. A pack GTIN does not identify each battery, and Article 13(7) has to be assessed before relying on packaging and accompanying documents. Serialisation is not required merely because a QR code is required. OQ-REG-001 and OQ-REG-003 remain relevant.

13.2 Rechargeable battery incorporated in an appliance ​

Assumed facts. A manufacturer places on the market a cordless appliance containing a rechargeable portable battery. The appliance and battery are distinct trade items. The product is not within an Article 11(2) derogation or Article 11(3) exclusion.

Regulatory outcome. The battery does not require a passport under Article 77(1), and Article 13(6)(b) applies from 18 February 2027. From that date, the person placing the product on the market ensures that the battery is readily removable and replaceable by the end-user and provides the required instructions and safety information (Article 11(1)–(3) and (7)–(8)). The manufacturer identifies the battery under Article 38(6), and Article 13(7) governs alternative placement.

GS1 implementation. GTIN identifies the appliance trade item, and a separate GTIN identifies the battery trade item. A model-to-model association supports product information; installation, service, safety investigation or recall may need a batch-level or instance-level relationship. A GS1 Digital Link URI for the battery connects to the Article 13(6)(b) information.

Limits. Battery attributes describe the battery, not the appliance, and the appliance identifier is not the battery identifier. OQ-REG-004 remains relevant.

Chapter 15: Data model for portable batteries ​

This chapter gives an overview of the information that exists about portable batteries under the Regulation, attribute by attribute. 15.1 How this overview was created ​

The list was built in four steps:

  1. Commission data model. The Commission’s battery data model, version 1.0.0 of 30 October 2026, is the starting point. It is official but is not a legal act. The names, definitions and data types of its attributes come from that model.
  2. Regulation. The list was checked against the consolidated text of the Regulation of 13 August 2026 and its secondary acts. Commission guidance on passport data points, version 3.2 of 19 October 2026, helped to interpret the data points. It states that it is not the Commission’s official position.
  3. Additions. Attributes the Regulation requires but the Commission’s model does not contain were added. No Commission data model exists for portable batteries, because they have no passport, so the list reuses the Commission’s terms where they fit.
  4. Check by battery type. Every attribute was checked for this battery type against the Regulation. This chapter keeps the attributes that apply to portable batteries and leaves out the rest.
